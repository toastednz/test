## Result

Cambie’s proposed classification is false for the eventual-orbit problem as stated. The explicit counterexample
\[
22\to32\to48\to64
\]
gives
\[
F^{k+2}(22)=2F^k(22)\qquad(k\ge1),
\]
although \(22\) is not in the proposed families. More generally, every \(2^{d+1}\cdot11\) and \(2^{d+1}\cdot43\) is an off-list solution with lag \(2\); there are also deeper predecessors such as \(2^{d+1}\cdot29\). Thus there are already infinitely many counterexamples to the conjectural list, generated by scaling a single transient predecessor. I also prove several Route 3 results: long-lag seeds must contain iterates with many distinct odd prime divisors; prime-support transitions obey an exact largest-prime descent law; all low-complexity support patterns for \(r=2\) recover the known primes \(3,5,7,47\); and the equation \(F^2(2p^t)=4p^t\) is reduced exactly to the stated shifted-totient obstruction. These results do not classify all direct seeds or all backward orbits, so the original problem remains unresolved.

## Complete Argument

### 1. Propagation from an even direct seed

For even \(m\),
\[
\phi(2m)=2\phi(m),
\]
and therefore
\[
F(2m)=2m+\phi(2m)=2F(m). \tag{1}
\]
Here \(m\) itself must be even; this is the case in all uses below.

If \(z\ge4\) is even, then all \(F^j(z)\) are even. Induction using (1) gives
\[
F^j(2z)=2F^j(z)\qquad(j\ge0). \tag{2}
\]
Consequently, if \(F^r(z)=2z\), then
\[
F^{j+r}(z)=F^j(2z)=2F^j(z)\qquad(j\ge0). \tag{3}
\]

Every power of two \(z=2^B\), \(B\ge2\), is a direct seed with lag \(2\). Indeed,
\[
F(2^B)=2^B+2^{B-1}=3\cdot2^{B-1},
\]
and
\[
\phi(3\cdot2^{B-1})=2^{B-1},
\]
so
\[
F^2(2^B)=4\cdot2^{B-1}=2^{B+1}=2z. \tag{4}
\]

### 2. An explicit counterexample to the proposed classification

Direct calculation gives
\[
22=2\cdot11,\qquad \phi(22)=10,
\]
and hence
\[
F(22)=32.
\]
Furthermore,
\[
32=2^5,\quad \phi(32)=16,\quad F(32)=48,
\]
and
\[
48=2^4\cdot3,\quad \phi(48)=16,\quad F(48)=64.
\]
Thus
\[
22\longmapsto32\longmapsto48\longmapsto64. \tag{5}
\]

By (4), \(32\) is a direct lag-\(2\) seed. Applying (3) to \(z=32\), for every \(j\ge0\),
\[
F^{j+2}(32)=2F^j(32).
\]
Setting \(j=k-1\) gives, for every \(k\ge1\),
\[
F^{k+2}(22)=2F^k(22). \tag{6}
\]

But \(22\) cannot be written as
\[
2^\ell q,\qquad q\in\{2,3,5,7,35,47\}.
\]
Its odd part is \(11\). Therefore (6) rigorously disproves Cambie’s proposed classification for starting values \(n\).

This is not merely one isolated counterexample. For every \(d\ge0\),
\[
n_d=2^d\cdot22=2^{d+1}\cdot11.
\]
Repeated application of (1) yields
\[
F(n_d)=2^dF(22)=2^{d+5}. \tag{7}
\]
The right side is a power of two and hence a direct lag-\(2\) seed. Therefore
\[
F^{k+2}(n_d)=2F^k(n_d)\qquad(k\ge1). \tag{8}
\]
Thus (8) supplies infinitely many off-list solutions.

### 3. A general prime-predecessor construction

Let \(A\ge3\) be odd and suppose
\[
p=\frac{2^A+1}{3}
\]
is prime. This is an integer because \(2^A\equiv-1\pmod3\). For every \(d\ge0\), set
\[
n=2^{d+1}p.
\]
Then
\[
\phi(n)=2^d(p-1),
\]
so
\[
F(n)=2^{d+1}p+2^d(p-1)
     =2^d(3p-1)
     =2^{A+d}. \tag{9}
\]
Again the image is a direct power-of-two seed. Hence
\[
F^{k+2}(n)=2F^k(n)\qquad(k\ge1). \tag{10}
\]

Two unconditional instances are
\[
A=5,\quad p=11,
\]
and
\[
A=7,\quad p=43.
\]
The latter gives another infinite off-list family
\[
n=2^{d+1}\cdot43.
\]

Backward chains can have more than one transient step. For example,
\[
58=2\cdot29,\quad \phi(58)=28,\quad F(58)=86,
\]
and
\[
86=2\cdot43,\quad \phi(86)=42,\quad F(86)=128.
\]
Thus
\[
58\longmapsto86\longmapsto128, \tag{11}
\]
and \(128\) is a direct lag-\(2\) seed. Consequently,
\[
F^{k+2}(58)=2F^k(58)\qquad(k\ge2). \tag{12}
\]
Scaling (11) by \(2^d\) proves that every
\[
2^{d+1}\cdot29
\]
is also an off-list eventual solution, now with \(K=2\).

More generally, if \(z_0=2^A\) and, recursively,
\[
p_j=\frac{z_{j-1}+1}{3}
\]
is prime and \(z_j=2p_j\), then
\[
F(z_j)=3p_j-1=z_{j-1}.
\]
Any such finite prime chain therefore gives an entire power-of-two-scaled family of transient starting values.

### 4. Route 3: density forces support complexity for long lags

For an integer \(N\), let \(\omega_o(N)\) denote the number of distinct odd prime divisors of \(N\). Let
\[
3=q_1<q_2<q_3<\cdots
\]
be the odd primes, and define
\[
c_s=\frac12\prod_{j=1}^s\left(1-\frac1{q_j}\right),\qquad c_0=\frac12. \tag{13}
\]

#### Lemma 1

If \(N\) is even and \(\omega_o(N)\le s\), then
\[
\frac{\phi(N)}N\ge c_s. \tag{14}
\]

#### Proof

Since \(N\) is even,
\[
\frac{\phi(N)}N
=\frac12\prod_{\substack{p\mid N\\p\text{ odd}}}
\left(1-\frac1p\right).
\]
Adding an odd prime factor decreases this product. For a fixed number of odd prime factors, replacing one of them by a smaller prime also decreases it, because \(1-1/p\) increases with \(p\). The minimum among supports with at most \(s\) odd primes is therefore attained at the first \(s\) odd primes, proving (14). ∎

Now suppose
\[
x_{i+1}=F(x_i),\qquad x_r=2x_0.
\]
Then
\[
2=\frac{x_r}{x_0}
=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}
\left(1+\frac{\phi(x_i)}{x_i}\right). \tag{15}
\]

#### Theorem 2

If
\[
(1+c_s)^r>2,
\]
then at least one of \(x_0,\dots,x_{r-1}\) has more than \(s\) distinct odd prime divisors.

#### Proof

Otherwise Lemma 1 would give
\[
1+\frac{\phi(x_i)}{x_i}\ge1+c_s
\]
for every \(i\). Multiplying and using (15),
\[
2\ge(1+c_s)^r>2,
\]
a contradiction. ∎

Two concrete consequences are useful.

For \(s=2\),
\[
c_2=\frac12\cdot\frac23\cdot\frac45=\frac4{15},
\]
and
\[
\left(1+\frac4{15}\right)^3
=\left(\frac{19}{15}\right)^3
=\frac{6859}{3375}>2.
\]
Therefore:

\[
r\ge3\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{some }x_i\text{ has at least three distinct odd prime divisors}. \tag{16}
\]

Such an \(x_i\) is at least
\[
2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7=210.
\]
Since \(x_i<2x_0\) for \(i<r\), (16) gives
\[
r\ge3\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_0>105. \tag{17}
\]

For \(s=5\),
\[
c_5
=\frac12\cdot\frac23\cdot\frac45\cdot\frac67
 \cdot\frac{10}{11}\cdot\frac{12}{13}
=\frac{192}{1001}.
\]
Thus
\[
1+c_5=\frac{1193}{1001}.
\]
An exact calculation gives
\[
1193^4-2\cdot1001^4=17\,625\,707\,999>0.
\]
Hence
\[
r\ge4\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\text{some }x_i\text{ has at least six distinct odd prime divisors}. \tag{18}
\]
The smallest possible radical of such an even number is
\[
2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13\cdot17
=510510.
\]
Consequently,
\[
r\ge4\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_0>255255,
\]
and since \(x_0\) is even,
\[
r\ge4\quad\Longrightarrow\quad x_0\ge255256. \tag{19}
\]

These bounds do not exclude long lags globally, but they show that every such lag forces substantial prime-support complexity along the orbit.

### 5. Exact largest-prime propagation

The following is the strongest elementary prime-propagation statement I found.

#### Lemma 3

Let \(P\) be an odd prime which is at least every prime divisor of every number in a finite orbit segment
\[
x_0,x_1,\dots,x_r.
\]
If \(e=v_P(x_i)\ge1\), then
\[
v_P(x_{i+1})=e-1. \tag{20}
\]

If \(P\nmid x_i\) but \(P^a\mid x_{i+1}\), then
\[
1+\prod_{q\mid x_i}(q-1)q^{-1}\equiv0\pmod{P^a}, \tag{21}
\]
where the inverses are taken modulo \(P^a\).

#### Proof

Factor
\[
x_i=\prod_q q^{e_q}.
\]
Because no prime divisor \(q\) of \(x_i\) exceeds \(P\), for every \(q<P\) one has \(P\nmid q-1\). Thus, if \(e=v_P(x_i)\ge1\),
\[
v_P(\phi(x_i))=e-1.
\]
The two summands \(x_i\) and \(\phi(x_i)\) have distinct \(P\)-adic valuations \(e\) and \(e-1\), so
\[
v_P(x_i+\phi(x_i))=e-1.
\]
This proves (20).

If \(P\nmid x_i\), then every prime divisor of \(x_i\) is invertible modulo \(P^a\). The condition \(P^a\mid x_i+\phi(x_i)\) can therefore be divided by \(x_i\), giving
\[
1+\frac{\phi(x_i)}{x_i}\equiv0\pmod{P^a}.
\]
Substituting
\[
\frac{\phi(x_i)}{x_i}
=\prod_{q\mid x_i}\frac{q-1}{q}
\]
gives (21). ∎

Thus the exponent of the largest prime decreases by one at every step while that prime remains present. It can return only through the support congruence (21). This is sharp rather than contradictory: for example,
\[
6\to8\to12
\]
loses \(3\) and then recovers it because
\[
1+\frac12\equiv0\pmod3.
\]
Similarly,
\[
10\to14\to20
\]
introduces \(7\) because
\[
1+\frac25\equiv0\pmod7.
\]
The congruence therefore explains the known transitions but does not prevent new large prime divisors.

### 6. Low-complexity support patterns for \(r=2\)

Suppose
\[
F^2(x)=2x,
\]
and put \(y=F(x)\). Then
\[
\left(1+\frac{\phi(x)}x\right)
\left(1+\frac{\phi(y)}y\right)=2. \tag{22}
\]
The ratios depend only on prime support.

If an even number has no odd prime divisor, its totient density is \(1/2\). If its only odd prime divisor is \(p\), its density is
\[
\frac{p-1}{2p},
\]
and its growth factor is
\[
\frac{3p-1}{2p}. \tag{23}
\]
If its odd prime support is exactly \(\{p,q\}\), its growth factor is
\[
\frac{3pq-p-q+1}{2pq}. \tag{24}
\]

#### Proposition 4

The following are the only \(r=2\) support signatures of the indicated forms:

1. If one of \(x,y\) has no odd prime divisor and the other has exactly one, that odd prime is \(3\).
2. If each has exactly one odd prime divisor, those primes are \(5\) and \(7\).
3. There is no signature in which one has no odd prime divisor and the other has exactly two.
4. If one has exactly one odd prime divisor and the other has exactly two, the supports are
   \[
   \{47\}\quad\text{and}\quad\{5,7\}.
   \]

#### Proof

For the first case, (22) and (23) give
\[
\frac32\cdot\frac{3p-1}{2p}=2,
\]
hence
\[
3(3p-1)=8p,
\]
so \(p=3\).

For the second case,
\[
(3p-1)(3q-1)=8pq.
\]
Rearranging gives
\[
(p-3)(q-3)=8.
\]
The positive even factor pairs of \(8\) are \(2\cdot4\) and \(4\cdot2\), so
\[
\{p,q\}=\{5,7\}.
\]

For the third case, let the two odd primes be \(p,q\). Equations (22) and (24) give
\[
3(3pq-p-q+1)=8pq,
\]
or
\[
(p-3)(q-3)=6.
\]
If either prime is \(3\), the left side is zero. Otherwise both factors are positive even integers, so their product is divisible by \(4\), whereas \(6\) is not. Hence no solutions exist.

For the fourth case, let \(\{p,q\}\) be the two-prime support and let \(s\) be the single prime. Equations (22)–(24) give
\[
(3pq-p-q+1)(3s-1)=8pqs.
\]
Writing
\[
D=3pq-p-q+1,\qquad
E=pq-3p-3q+3,
\]
this becomes
\[
sE=D. \tag{25}
\]
Since \(D>0\), one must have \(E>0\), and
\[
s=\frac DE
=3+\frac{8(p+q-1)}E. \tag{26}
\]
Assume \(p<q\). Since \(p,q,s\) are odd, \(E\) is even and \(s-3\) is a positive even integer. Thus (26) implies
\[
\frac{8(p+q-1)}E\ge2,
\]
or
\[
E\le4(p+q-1).
\]
Equivalently,
\[
(p-7)(q-7)\le42. \tag{27}
\]

The case \(p=3\) gives \(E=-6\), impossible.

If \(p=5\), equation (26) becomes
\[
s=7+\frac{40}{q-6}.
\]
Since \(q-6\) is a positive odd divisor of \(40\), \(q=7\) or \(11\). These give \(s=47\) and \(15\), respectively, so only
\[
(p,q,s)=(5,7,47)
\]
survives.

If \(p=7\), then
\[
s=5+\frac{42}{2q-9}.
\]
The positive odd divisors of \(42\) are \(1,3,7,21\), producing no prime \(q>7\).

If \(p\ge11\), (27) leaves only
\[
(p,q)=(11,13)\quad\text{or}\quad(11,17).
\]
For these,
\[
s=\frac{406}{74}=\frac{203}{37}
\]
and
\[
s=\frac{534}{106}=\frac{267}{53},
\]
neither an integer. This proves the proposition. ∎

Thus all \(r=2\) seeds outside the known support signatures must have substantially more complicated supports, for example at least two odd primes on both sides, or at least three on one side.

### 7. Exact analysis of \(F^2(2p^t)=4p^t\)

#### Proposition 5

Let \(p\) be an odd prime and \(t\ge1\). Then
\[
F^2(2p^t)=4p^t
\]
if and only if \(t=1\) and one of the following holds:

1. \(p=3\);
2. \(p=5\);
3. \(p=8m+7\) and
   \[
   \phi(6m+5)=4m+4. \tag{28}
   \]

#### Proof

Let \(x=2p^t\). Then
\[
\phi(x)=p^{t-1}(p-1),
\]
and hence
\[
F(x)=p^{t-1}(3p-1). \tag{29}
\]
The equation \(F^2(x)=4p^t\) is therefore equivalent to
\[
\phi\!\left(p^{t-1}(3p-1)\right)
=p^{t-1}(p+1). \tag{30}
\]

Suppose first that \(t\ge2\). Since
\[
\gcd(p,3p-1)=1,
\]
equation (30) becomes
\[
p^{t-2}(p-1)\phi(3p-1)=p^{t-1}(p+1),
\]
or
\[
(p-1)\phi(3p-1)=p(p+1). \tag{31}
\]
Thus \(p-1\mid p(p+1)\). Reducing modulo \(p-1\) gives \(p(p+1)\equiv2\pmod{p-1}\), so \(p-1\mid2\). Since \(p\) is odd, this forces \(p=3\). But substitution in (31) gives
\[
2\phi(8)=8\ne12=3\cdot4.
\]
Therefore \(t\ge2\) is impossible.

Now let \(t=1\). Equation (30) becomes
\[
\phi(3p-1)=p+1. \tag{32}
\]

If \(p\equiv3\pmod8\), then \(v_2(p+1)=2\), while \(8\mid3p-1\). Write \(a=v_2(3p-1)\ge3\). The standard formula gives
\[
v_2(\phi(3p-1))
=a-1+\sum_{\substack{q\mid3p-1\\q\text{ odd}}}v_2(q-1).
\]
For this valuation to equal \(2\), one must have \(a=3\) and no odd prime divisor. Hence \(3p-1=8\), so \(p=3\).

If \(p\equiv1\) or \(5\pmod8\), then
\[
v_2(3p-1)=v_2(p+1)=1.
\]
Writing \(3p-1=2M\) with \(M\) odd, equation (32) implies
\[
v_2(\phi(M))=1.
\]
Therefore \(M=q^a\) for a single prime \(q\equiv3\pmod4\). Thus
\[
3p-1=2q^a,\qquad q^{a-1}(q-1)=p+1.
\]
Eliminating \(p\) gives
\[
q^{a-1}(q-3)=4. \tag{33}
\]
Since \(q\equiv3\pmod4\), the only possibility is \(q=7,a=1\), yielding \(p=5\). Thus there are no solutions with \(p\equiv1\pmod8\), and \(p=5\) is the only \(5\bmod8\) solution.

Finally, if \(p=8m+7\), then
\[
3p-1=24m+20=4(6m+5),
\]
where \(6m+5\) is odd. Hence
\[
\phi(3p-1)=2\phi(6m+5).
\]
Equation (32) is exactly
\[
2\phi(6m+5)=8m+8,
\]
which is (28). The preceding implications are reversible, proving the proposition. ∎

For \(p=7\), equation (28) is \(\phi(5)=4\); for \(p=47\), it is \(\phi(35)=24\). Proposition 4 further shows that any other solution in this \(7\bmod8\) branch must make \(6m+5\) divisible by at least three distinct odd primes.

In terms of \(u=6m+5\), equation (28) is
\[
\phi(u)=\frac23(u+1),
\]
which is precisely the difficult inverse-totient obstruction identified in the brief.

## Self-Audit

1. **This does not classify all pairs \((n,r)\).** It rigorously disproves the proposed list, but the brief correctly states that one or even infinitely many new families do not solve the original classification problem. The claimed refutation nevertheless holds because the exact orbit \(22\to32\to48\to64\), together with the propagation identity, proves the required relation for every \(k\ge1\).

2. **The support-signature results cover only explicitly stated low-complexity cases.** In particular, Proposition 4 does not eliminate signatures with two odd primes on both iterates or with three or more odd primes. I believe every stated case is complete because each is reduced to an exact finite Diophantine calculation, but no extrapolation beyond those cases is intended.

3. **Lemma 3 requires \(P\) to be largest over the entire orbit segment, not merely a largest prime of one iterate.** A larger prime \(q\equiv1\pmod P\) would contribute an extra factor of \(P\) to \(\phi(x_i)\) and invalidate the conclusion. The lemma is valid under its stated global hypothesis because then every other prime \(q\) satisfies \(q-1<P\).

## Route Diagnosis

**What Route 3 achieved.**

- The density product gives rigorous support-complexity bounds: \(r\ge3\) forces an iterate with at least three odd prime divisors, while \(r\ge4\) forces at least six.
- Largest-prime exponents obey an exact descent law, and the only mechanism for reintroduction is the explicit support congruence (21).
- All \(r=2\) density signatures involving at most one odd prime on one side and at most two on the other recover exactly \(3,5,7,47\).
- For odd-prime-power cores \(2p^t\), the \(r=2\) equation forces \(t=1\) and reduces exactly to the shifted-totient equation
  \[
  \phi(u)=\frac23(u+1).
  \]

**Where Route 3 is blocked.**

The support of \(F(x)=x+\phi(x)\) is not controlled by the support of \(x\). Already
\[
F(2p)=3p-1
\]
can lose \(p\), introduce a larger prime, become a power of two, or split into several unrelated primes. The largest-prime congruence is necessary but flexible enough to realize all known seeds. The density identity is also fundamentally telescoping:
\[
\prod_i\left(1+\frac{\phi(x_i)}{x_i}\right)=\frac{x_r}{x_0},
\]
so without a new theorem controlling the additive factorization of \(x+\phi(x)\), it does not force \(r=2\). In the \(r=2\), one-prime-support branch it reaches the unresolved inverse-totient equation above, a block of comparable strength to the original hard component.

**Superior alternative that emerged.**

The backward-orbit route immediately finds \(22\to32\), disproving the proposed starting-value classification. It also reveals prime chains such as
\[
