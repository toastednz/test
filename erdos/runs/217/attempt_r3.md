STATUS: BLOCKED

## Result

Route 3 yields several rigorous geometric filters on the distance-colored complete graph, but not a classification. The strongest new mechanism is an exact affine-rank obstruction: whenever two pairs of opposite edges of a four-point set have matching colors, the four points must form a parallelogram, unless they are concyclic. Thus every admissible coloring generates explicit linear equations among the point coordinates; if their row space has rank at least \(n-2\), or contains a nonzero vector supported on at most three vertices, realization in general position is impossible. This completely eliminates the natural “difference coloring” for every \(n\ge5\), even though all its monochromatic graphs are linear forests and hence evade the usual degree and \(K_{2,3}\) tests. I also prove exact constraints generated by monochromatic diamonds and rhombi, and classify possible color-preserving automorphisms: for odd \(n\ge5\) the colored graph must be asymmetric, while for even \(n\) its only possible nontrivial symmetry is a fixed-point-free half-turn satisfying strong parity conditions. The route remains blocked because the multiplicity spectrum alone does not presently force enough parallelogram, diamond, rhombus, or symmetry structure to trigger these obstructions for every \(n\ge9\).

## Complete Argument

### 1. Basic colored-graph accounting

Color the edge \(\{p,q\}\) of \(K_n\) by its Euclidean distance. There are \(n-1\) colors, whose class sizes are \(1,\dots,n-1\).

For a distance \(r\), let \(G_r\) be its color graph and let \(d_r(v)\) be the degree of \(v\) in \(G_r\).

#### Lemma 1.1: Local degree and common-neighbor bounds

For every \(r\):

1. \(\Delta(G_r)\le3\).
2. \(G_r\) contains no \(K_{2,3}\).
3. For every pair \(x,y\in P\), there are at most two points \(v\in P\setminus\{x,y\}\) satisfying
   \[
   |vx|=|vy|.
   \]

**Proof.**

1. If \(v\) had four neighbors at distance \(r\), those four neighbors would lie on the circle of radius \(r\) centered at \(v\), contradicting no four concyclic.
2. Three common neighbors of two vertices \(u,v\) in \(G_r\) would be three common intersection points of the two distinct radius-\(r\) circles centered at \(u\) and \(v\). Two distinct circles have at most two common points.
3. Every point equidistant from \(x\) and \(y\) lies on the perpendicular bisector of \(xy\). Three such selected points would be collinear. ∎

Let
\[
I=\sum_r\sum_{v\in P}\binom{d_r(v)}2.
\]
This is the number of unordered pairs of equal-length edges sharing a vertex, equivalently the number of isosceles-triangle apex incidences. Equilateral triangles contribute three incidences.

#### Lemma 1.2: Bounds on adjacent equal-edge pairs

For every desired configuration,
\[
\left\lfloor\frac{(n-1)^2}{4}\right\rfloor
\le I\le nF(n-1),
\]
where
\[
F(N)=
\begin{cases}
N,&3\mid N,\\
N-1,&3\nmid N.
\end{cases}
\]

In particular, \(I=O(n^2)\).

**Proof.**

Consider a color graph with \(m\) edges. If \(t\) of its vertices are nonisolated, then
\[
\sum_v\binom{d(v)}2
\ge \sum_{d(v)>0}(d(v)-1)
=2m-t
\ge2m-n.
\]
The left side is nonnegative, so it is at least \(\max(0,2m-n)\). Summing over class sizes \(m=1,\dots,n-1\) gives
\[
I\ge\sum_{m=1}^{n-1}\max(0,2m-n)
=\left\lfloor\frac{(n-1)^2}{4}\right\rfloor.
\]

For the upper bound, fix a vertex \(v\). We have
\[
\sum_r d_r(v)=n-1,\qquad d_r(v)\in\{0,1,2,3\}.
\]
For \(d=0,1,2,3\), the values of \(\binom d2\) are \(0,0,1,3\). Packing the incident edges into degree-three color incidences maximizes the sum. If \(N=n-1\) is divisible by \(3\), the maximum is \(N\). Otherwise at least one degree-one or degree-two remainder is necessary, costing one, and the maximum is \(N-1\). Thus
\[
\sum_r\binom{d_r(v)}2\le F(n-1).
\]
Sum over \(v\). ∎

There is also the independent geometric upper bound
\[
I\le 2\binom n2=n(n-1),
\]
because each base \(xy\) has at most two isosceles apexes by Lemma 1.1.

The spectrum gives
\[
\sum_r\binom{\mu(r)}2=\binom n3.
\]
Every pair of distinct equal-length edges is either adjacent or disjoint. Hence, if \(D\) denotes the number of disjoint equal-edge pairs,
\[
D=\binom n3-I.
\]
Thus almost all equal-edge pairs are disjoint:
\[
D=\binom n3-O(n^2).
\]
This observation alone gives no contradiction because such pairs can be spread among the \(\binom n4\) four-point subsets.

### 2. Four-point metric rigidity

The first useful cross-color obstruction occurs when a four-point set has two equalities between opposite pairs of edges.

#### Lemma 2.1: Opposite equalities force a parallelogram or a circle

Let \(A,B,C,D\) be four distinct points with no three collinear. If
\[
AB=CD,\qquad AC=BD,
\]
then exactly one of the following holds:

1. \(A,B,C,D\) are concyclic;
2. they satisfy
   \[
   A+D=B+C,
   \]
   so \(A,B,D,C\), in that order, is a parallelogram.

Consequently, under the no-four-concyclic hypothesis, the affine equation
\[
A+D-B-C=0
\]
is forced.

**Proof.**

Apply a rigid motion and write
\[
A=(0,0),\qquad D=(L,0),\qquad L>0,
\]
and
\[
B=(x,y),\qquad C=(u,v).
\]
Because \(A,B,D\) are not collinear, \(y\ne0\).

The two hypotheses give
\[
x^2+y^2=(u-L)^2+v^2
\]
and
\[
u^2+v^2=(x-L)^2+y^2.
\]
Subtracting the second equality from the first yields
\[
2Lx-L^2=L^2-2Lu,
\]
hence
\[
u=L-x.
\]
Substitution then gives \(v^2=y^2\).

If \(v=-y\), then
\[
B+C=(x,y)+(L-x,-y)=(L,0)=A+D,
\]
which is the parallelogram alternative.

If \(v=y\), then \(C=(L-x,y)\), the reflection of \(B\) across the perpendicular bisector of \(AD\). Every circle through \(A\) and \(D\) has its center on this perpendicular bisector, so the circle through \(A,D,B\) also contains \(C\). Thus the four points are concyclic. ∎

#### Corollary 2.2: Three opposite matching equalities are forbidden

For four vertices \(A,B,C,D\), it is impossible that all three equalities
\[
AB=CD,\qquad AC=BD,\qquad AD=BC
\]
hold.

**Proof.**

The first two equalities, together with no four concyclic, make \(A,B,D,C\) a parallelogram. In this parallelogram, \(AD\) and \(BC\) are the diagonals. The third equality says the diagonals are equal. A parallelogram with equal diagonals is a rectangle, and every rectangle is cyclic, contradicting the hypothesis. ∎

This is a genuinely cross-color condition: the three common values need not be the same distance.

### 3. The affine row-space obstruction

For every instance of Lemma 2.1, introduce the row vector
\[
\rho_{A,B,C,D}=e_A+e_D-e_B-e_C\in\mathbb R^n.
\]
Let \(R\) be the matrix consisting of all such forced rows.

Write the point coordinates as column vectors
\[
x=(x_1,\dots,x_n)^T,\qquad y=(y_1,\dots,y_n)^T.
\]

#### Lemma 3.1: Rank and support obstruction

Every realizable coloring in the required general position satisfies:

1. \(\operatorname{rank}R\le n-3\);
2. the row space of \(R\) contains no nonzero vector supported on at most three coordinates.

**Proof.**

Every row is a forced affine equation, so
\[
R\mathbf 1=0,\qquad Rx=0,\qquad Ry=0.
\]
The three vectors \(\mathbf1,x,y\) are linearly independent. Otherwise there would be constants \(\alpha,\beta,\gamma\), not all zero, with
\[
\alpha+\beta x_i+\gamma y_i=0
\]
for every \(i\), placing all points on one line. Hence the nullity of \(R\) is at least three, proving
\[
\operatorname{rank}R\le n-3.
\]

Now suppose a nonzero vector \(\lambda\) in the row space has support at most three. Every row of \(R\) has coordinate sum zero, so
\[
\sum_i\lambda_i=0.
\]
Also
\[
\sum_i\lambda_i p_i=0.
\]
If the support has size two, these equations force two selected points to be equal. If it has size three, they give a nontrivial affine dependence among three selected points, making them collinear. Both conclusions are forbidden. ∎

This is an exact rational-linear-algebra test on an abstract coloring, before solving any nonlinear Euclidean system.

### 4. The canonical abstract coloring and why pure monochromatic graph theory fails

For every \(n\), define a coloring of \(K_n\) on vertices
\[
0,1,\dots,n-1
\]
by
\[
c(\{i,j\})=|i-j|.
\]
The color \(d\) occurs exactly \(n-d\) times, so the class sizes are precisely \(1,\dots,n-1\).

For fixed \(d\), the graph consists of paths
\[
r,\ r+d,\ r+2d,\dots
\]
for the residue classes \(r\bmod d\). Thus every color graph is a linear forest. In particular:

- every monochromatic degree is at most \(2\);
- there is no monochromatic cycle;
- there is no monochromatic triangle, \(K_{2,3}\), \(K_4-e\), or \(K_4\).

Therefore the ordinary monochromatic forbidden-subgraph conditions admit abstract colorings for arbitrarily large \(n\).

Nevertheless, the affine obstruction eliminates this coloring geometrically.

#### Proposition 4.1: The difference coloring is unrealizable for \(n\ge5\)

No point set in the required general position realizes the coloring
\[
c(ij)=|i-j|
\]
when \(n\ge5\).

**Proof.**

For each \(i=0,\dots,n-4\), the four consecutive vertices satisfy
\[
|p_i-p_{i+1}|=|p_{i+2}-p_{i+3}|
\]
and
\[
|p_i-p_{i+2}|=|p_{i+1}-p_{i+3}|.
\]
If these four points were concyclic, the configuration would already be forbidden. Otherwise Lemma 2.1 gives
\[
p_i+p_{i+3}=p_{i+1}+p_{i+2}.
\]
For \(i=0,1\), adding the two equations yields
\[
p_0-2p_2+p_4=0.
\]
Thus \(p_0,p_2,p_4\) are collinear, with \(p_2\) their midpoint, contradicting no three collinear. ∎

The threshold is sharp for this coloring. For \(n=4\), the points
\[
p_0=(0,1),\quad p_1=(1,-1),\quad
p_2=(2,1),\quad p_3=(3,-1)
\]
have squared distances
\[
5,5,5;\qquad 4,4;\qquad 13
\]
for lags \(1,2,3\), respectively. Every three-point determinant is \(\pm4\), and the four points form a nonrectangular parallelogram in the order \(p_0,p_2,p_3,p_1\), hence are not cyclic.

### 5. Monochromatic diamonds and rhombi

These give additional relations between distinct distance colors.

#### Lemma 5.1: Diamond propagation

Suppose \(G_r\) contains a \(K_4-e\), with central edge \(uv\), outer vertices \(x,y\), and all five present edges of length \(r\). Then
\[
xy=\sqrt3\,r.
\]

Moreover:

1. all diamonds in \(G_r\) have their missing edges in the single distance class \(\sqrt3\,r\);
2. distinct diamonds in \(G_r\) have distinct missing edges;
3. if colors are regarded as their numerical distances, the partial map
   \[
   r\longmapsto \sqrt3\,r
   \]
   induced by diamonds is injective and acyclic;
4. the number of diamonds in \(G_r\) is at most \(\mu(\sqrt3r)\le n-1\).

**Proof.**

Normalize
\[
u=(-r/2,0),\qquad v=(r/2,0).
\]
The common intersections of the radius-\(r\) circles centered at \(u,v\) are
\[
(0,\sqrt3r/2),\qquad (0,-\sqrt3r/2).
\]
Since \(x,y\) are distinct common neighbors, they are these two points, so
\[
xy=\sqrt3r.
\]

Conversely, once \(x,y\) and \(r\) are fixed with \(xy=\sqrt3r\), the possible central vertices are exactly the two intersections of the radius-\(r\) circles centered at \(x,y\). Thus the missing edge determines the diamond uniquely, proving injectivity on diamonds.

If two source distances have the same target, then
\[
\sqrt3r=\sqrt3r',
\]
so \(r=r'\). Finally, repeated multiplication by \(\sqrt3>1\) cannot form a directed cycle. ∎

The diamond itself is not forbidden. For \(r=1\), the coordinates
\[
(-1/2,0),\quad (1/2,0),\quad
(0,\sqrt3/2),\quad (0,-\sqrt3/2)
\]
are in the required general position.

#### Lemma 5.2: Rhombus relation

If
\[
AB=BC=CD=DA=r,
\]
then \(ABCD\) is a parallelogram and
\[
AC^2+BD^2=4r^2.
\]
Under no four concyclic,
\[
AC\ne BD.
\]

**Proof.**

The points \(B,D\) are the two common intersections of the radius-\(r\) circles centered at \(A,C\). Put
\[
A=(-a,0),\quad C=(a,0),\quad
B=(0,h),\quad D=(0,-h).
\]
Then \(r^2=a^2+h^2\), while
\[
AC=2a,\qquad BD=2h.
\]
Therefore
\[
AC^2+BD^2=4(a^2+h^2)=4r^2.
\]
If \(AC=BD\), then \(a=h\), so all four points lie on the circle centered at the origin. ∎

Thus every monochromatic four-cycle creates a rational linear equation among the squared-distance variables:
\[
s_{\text{diag }1}+s_{\text{diag }2}=4s_{\text{side}}.
\]
If one diagonal also has length \(r\), the other has length \(\sqrt3r\), recovering the diamond relation.

### 6. Color-preserving automorphisms

A color-preserving automorphism is a permutation \(\sigma\) of the points such that every edge retains its distance color. Because all color multiplicities are distinct, even an automorphism allowed initially to permute color names must fix every color class.

#### Theorem 6.1: Possible automorphism groups

Let \(n\ge5\). The color-preserving automorphism group of a desired configuration is either:

1. trivial; or
2. for even \(n\), a group of order two generated by a fixed-point-free half-turn.

For odd \(n\ge5\), it must be trivial.

**Proof.**

Any color-preserving permutation preserves every pairwise distance. Choose three noncollinear points. There is a unique Euclidean isometry taking them to their prescribed images, and preservation of distances to these three points forces the same isometry to realize the permutation on all of \(P\).

Because the permutation has finite order and the set contains three noncollinear points, the corresponding isometry also has finite order. Hence it is a reflection or a finite-order rotation.

A reflection cannot preserve such a set of at least five points. Put the reflection axis on the \(x\)-axis. There are at most two fixed selected points on the axis. If there are two distinct reflected pairs
\[
(a,\pm b),\qquad(c,\pm d),
\]
then:

- if \(a=c\), three of these points are collinear;
- if \(a\ne c\), choose \(h\) so that
  \[
  (a-h)^2+b^2=(c-h)^2+d^2.
  \]
  The circle centered at \((h,0)\) contains all four points.

Thus there is at most one reflected pair and at most two fixed points, giving at most four selected points.

A rotation of order at least four gives an orbit of at least four noncentral points on one circle, also forbidden.

For a rotation of order three, no unordered edge is fixed by the rotation: a two-element set cannot support a nontrivial action of a group of order three, and a nontrivial rotation has only one fixed point. Hence every edge orbit has size three, so every distance multiplicity is divisible by three. This contradicts the existence of the distance class of multiplicity one.

The only remaining possibility is a half-turn. If its center belonged to \(P\), every noncentral pair \(p,-p\), together with the center, would be collinear. Thus the half-turn has no fixed selected point, and \(n\) is even.

Finally, two distinct half-turns compose to a nonzero translation. No finite nonempty point set is invariant under a nonzero translation. Therefore there is at most one nonidentity automorphism. ∎

#### Corollary 6.2: Parity structure under a half-turn

Suppose \(n=2k\) and the coloring has a nontrivial automorphism. After translating the center to the origin,
\[
P=\{\pm v_1,\dots,\pm v_k\}.
\]
Then:

1. the \(k\) radii \(|v_i|\) are pairwise distinct;
2. no \(v_i,v_j\) are parallel for \(i\ne j\);
3. every odd-sized distance class contains exactly one diameter edge \(\{v_i,-v_i\}\);
4. every even-sized class contains no diameter edge;
5. after deleting the diameter edge from each odd class and quotienting the remaining edges by the half-turn, the class sizes become
   \[
   0,1,1,2,2,\dots,k-1,k-1.
   \]

**Proof.**

Equal radii would place \(\pm v_i,\pm v_j\) on one circle. Parallel vectors would put at least three of the four points \(\pm v_i,\pm v_j\) on one line.

The only edges fixed setwise by the half-turn are the \(k\) diameter edges. Every other edge lies in a two-element orbit. Thus a distance class has odd size exactly when it contains an odd number of diameter edges.

Among \(1,\dots,2k-1\), exactly \(k\) class sizes are odd. Each needs at least one fixed edge, and there are exactly \(k\) fixed edges total. Hence each odd class contains exactly one and each even class none. Dividing the remaining multiplicity by two gives
\[
\left\lfloor\frac m2\right\rfloor,\qquad m=1,\dots,2k-1,
\]
which is the asserted list. ∎

This reduction is exact but does not yet contradict Euclidean realizability.

## Self-Audit

1. **The parallelogram equations are sensitive to the labeling of the four vertices.**  
   A wrong pairing would invalidate the row-space test. The coordinate proof of Lemma 2.1 explicitly derives \(A+D=B+C\) from precisely \(AB=CD\) and \(AC=BD\), and the computational pseudocode below handles all three pairings separately.

2. **The automorphism theorem assumes every color automorphism extends to a plane isometry.**  
   This is justified because three noncollinear points determine a unique Euclidean isometry, and a fourth point is uniquely determined by its distances to those three noncollinear reference points. Thus preservation of the full colored complete graph is enough.

3. **The strongest filters do not apply to every abstract coloring.**  
   This is the actual block, not a hidden proof gap. The spectrum does not presently force a high-rank family of parallelogram equations, any diamond or rhombus, or a nontrivial automorphism. Therefore none of the proved lemmas establishes nonexistence for \(n\ge9\).

The affine-row filter for an arbitrary edge coloring can be implemented exactly as follows. Here `col[(i,j)]` is the color of edge \(ij\).

```python
from itertools import combinations
from sympy import Matrix, zeros

def edge(i, j):
    return (i, j) if i < j else (j, i)

def affine_rows(n, col):
    rows = []

    for a, b, c, d in combinations(range(n), 4):
        # The three perfect-matching equalities.
        m1 = col[edge(a,b)] == col[edge(c,d)]
        m2 = col[edge(a,c)] == col[edge(b,d)]
        m3 = col[edge(a,d)] == col[edge(b,c)]

        if m1 and m2 and m3:
            return None, "forbidden three matching equalities"

        if m1 and m2:
            # p_a + p_d = p_b + p_c
            r = [0]*n
            r[a] += 1; r[d] += 1
            r[b] -= 1; r[c] -= 1
            rows.append(r)

        if m1 and m3:
            # p_a + p_c = p_b + p_d
            r = [0]*n
            r[a] += 1; r[c] += 1
            r[b] -= 1; r[d] -= 1
            rows.append(r)

        if m2 and m3:
            # p_a + p_b = p_c + p_d
            r = [0]*n
            r[a] += 1; r[b] += 1
            r[c] -= 1; r[d] -= 1
            rows.append(r)

    R = Matrix(rows) if rows else zeros(0, n)
    return R, None

def affine_obstruction(n, col):
    R, reason = affine_rows(n, col)
    if reason:
        return reason

    rho = R.rank()
    if rho > n - 3:
        return f"rank obstruction: rank {rho} > {n-3}"

    # There is a nonzero row-space vector supported inside S iff
    # rank(R) > rank(R restricted to columns outside S).
    for size in (2, 3):
        for S in combinations(range(n), size):
            outside = [i for i in range(n) if i not in S]
            Rout = R[:, outside]
            if rho > Rout.rank():
                return f"row-space vector supported on {S}"

    return None
```

## Route Diagnosis

**Proved ledger.**

- Exact lower and upper bounds on the total number of adjacent equal-edge pairs.
- Opposite matching equalities force either concyclicity or a specific parallelogram equation.
- Three matching equalities on one four-set are impossible.
- The resulting affine equation matrix must have rank at most \(n-3\) and row-space minimum support at least four.
- The difference coloring, despite having only monochromatic linear forests, is impossible for every \(n\ge5\).
- Monochromatic diamonds induce an injective acyclic distance-ratio map \(r\mapsto\sqrt3r\).
- Monochromatic rhombi impose exact linear equations on squared distance values.
- For odd \(n\ge5\), the colored graph must be asymmetric. For even \(n\), its only possible nontrivial automorphism is a fixed-point-free half-turn with the stated parity quotient.

**Plausible but unproved.**

- A sufficiently large spectrum coloring may necessarily produce either a forbidden affine row space or an inconsistent network of diamond/rhombus equations.
- The centrally symmetric quotient
  \[
  0,1,1,2,2,\dots,k-1,k-1
  \]
  may itself be impossible for large \(k\).
- SAT enumeration at \(n=9\) may become manageable after affine-rank postprocessing.

**Dead ends.**

- Degree-\(3\), \(K_{2,3}\), and ordinary monochromatic forbidden-subgraph arguments cannot suffice: the difference coloring satisfies much stronger monochromatic conditions for every \(n\).
- The adjacent equal-edge count remains between constants times \(n^2\), while the required equal-distance energy is of order \(n^3\); disjoint equal segments remain uncontrolled.
- Automorphism restrictions do not address asymmetric colorings.
- Four-point parallelogram constraints are powerful when present, but the spectrum does not currently force two matching equalities on enough four-sets.

The precise block is therefore a missing universal structural theorem converting the global class-size spectrum \(1,\dots,n-1\) into one of the proved metric obstructions. A fresh Route 3 attempt should combine exhaustive \(n=9\) coloring enumeration with the affine row-space and squared-distance filters above, then inspect surviving motif-poor colorings for a general pattern.
