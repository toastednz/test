## Result

Route 3 does not currently yield a universal finite \(k\). I proved a compactness-compatible finite theorem when the progression core has bounded local directional complexity: if each relevant point sees at most \(t\) unit directions among relevant points, then all sufficiently large \(k=O(t\log^2 k)\) admit an independent transversal. Consequently, any finite obstruction for large \(k\) must contain a relevant point incident with at least \(k/(256\log^2 k+8/k)\) distinct unit directions, and its relevant unit-distance graph must be non-bipartite. However, this local directional complexity is unbounded even in progression cores, so the theorem does not imply an upper bound for the plane. I also give exact counterexamples to two natural strategies: choosing a maximum independent set, and constructing the coloring from a finite additive quotient.

## Complete Argument

### 1. Reduction to the progression core

Let \(V\subset\mathbb R^2\) be finite, and let \(\mathcal P\) be a finite family of unit-step \(k\)-term progressions contained in \(V\). Define the progression core
\[
C=\bigcup_{P\in\mathcal P}P.
\]

Points of \(V\setminus C\) may always be colored blue. They occur in no positive progression clause and therefore do not help meet any progression. Once they are blue, they cannot participate in a red-red unit pair. Thus it is enough to find a red set \(R\subseteq C\) which is independent in the unit-distance graph induced by \(C\) and intersects every \(P\in\mathcal P\).

This pruning is important: high unit degree caused only by points outside all progressions is irrelevant.

For \(v\in C\), define its local directional complexity
\[
t_C(v)=
\left|
 \left\{
 \{\pm u\}:
 \|u\|_2=1,\quad v+u\in C\text{ or }v-u\in C
 \right\}
\right|,
\]
and put
\[
t=\max_{v\in C}t_C(v).
\]

For each unoriented direction \(\{\pm u\}\), there are at most two unit neighbors \(v+u,v-u\). Hence
\[
\deg_{G_{\rm unit}[C]}(v)\le 2t. \tag{1}
\]

Also, a fixed \(v\) lies in at most \(k\) distinct \(k\)-term progressions in any one unoriented direction: \(v\) can occupy one of the \(k\) possible positions, and that position determines the initial point. Therefore
\[
|\{P\in\mathcal P:v\in P\}|\le kt. \tag{2}
\]

Duplicate progression clauses, including those obtained by reversing direction, are of course counted only once.

---

### 2. Bipartite progression cores are automatically satisfiable

**Proposition 1.**  
If the unit-distance graph induced by \(C\) is bipartite, then the finite system is satisfiable for every \(k\ge2\).

**Proof.** In each connected component of \(G_{\rm unit}[C]\), choose one bipartition class and color it red; color the other class blue. Color \(V\setminus C\) blue.

The red set is independent. Every progression \(P\in\mathcal P\) contains consecutive unit-distance pairs, so its vertices lie in one connected component and alternate between the two bipartition classes. In particular, its first two vertices include a red vertex. Thus every progression is hit. ∎

Consequently, every finite obstruction has a non-bipartite unit-distance graph on its progression core.

### 3. A bounded-direction independent-transversal theorem

I use the following standard asymmetric Lovász local lemma, with its proof included for completeness.

**Lemma 2: Asymmetric local lemma.**  
Let \(A_1,\dots,A_N\) be events with a dependency graph, meaning that \(A_i\) is independent of the sigma-algebra generated by its non-neighbors. Suppose numbers \(x_i\in(0,1)\) satisfy
\[
\Pr(A_i)\le x_i\prod_{j\sim i}(1-x_j)
\]
for every \(i\). Then
\[
\Pr\left(\bigcap_{i=1}^N\overline{A_i}\right)>0.
\]

**Proof.** We first prove, by induction on \(|S|\), that
\[
\Pr\left(A_i\;\middle|\;\bigcap_{j\in S}\overline{A_j}\right)\le x_i
\]
whenever \(i\notin S\) and the conditioning event has positive probability.

Write \(S_1=S\cap\Gamma(i)\), where \(\Gamma(i)\) is the neighborhood of \(i\) in the dependency graph, and \(S_2=S\setminus S_1\). Since \(A_i\) is independent of the events indexed by \(S_2\),
\[
\Pr\left(A_i\;\middle|\;\bigcap_{j\in S}\overline{A_j}\right)
\le
\frac{\Pr(A_i)}
{\Pr\left(\bigcap_{j\in S_1}\overline{A_j}\,\middle|\,
\bigcap_{j\in S_2}\overline{A_j}\right)}.
\]
Expanding the denominator one event at a time and applying the induction hypothesis gives
\[
\Pr\left(\bigcap_{j\in S_1}\overline{A_j}\,\middle|\,
\bigcap_{j\in S_2}\overline{A_j}\right)
\ge \prod_{j\in S_1}(1-x_j).
\]
Therefore
\[
\Pr\left(A_i\;\middle|\;\bigcap_{j\in S}\overline{A_j}\right)
\le
\frac{x_i\prod_{j\in\Gamma(i)}(1-x_j)}
{\prod_{j\in S_1}(1-x_j)}
\le x_i.
\]
Finally, reveal the complements \(\overline{A_i}\) one at a time. Their joint probability is at least
\[
\prod_{i=1}^N(1-x_i)>0.
\]
∎

Here is the finite theorem obtained from it.

**Theorem 3.**  
Let \(k\ge32\), and let \(V,\mathcal P,C,t\) be as above. If
\[
\frac{64t(\log k)^2}{k}+\frac{2t}{k^2}\le\frac14, \tag{3}
\]
then there is an independent set \(R\subseteq C\) meeting every \(P\in\mathcal P\).

**Proof.** Independently color every point of \(C\) red with probability
\[
p=\frac{4\log k}{k};
\]
otherwise color it blue. Since \(k\ge32\), we have \(0<p<1/2\).

There are two types of bad events:

1. For each unit pair \(e=\{v,w\}\subseteq C\), let \(E_e\) be the event that both endpoints are red. Then
   \[
   \Pr(E_e)=p^2.
   \]

2. For each \(P\in\mathcal P\), let \(F_P\) be the event that all its \(k\) vertices are blue. Then
   \[
   \Pr(F_P)=(1-p)^k\le e^{-pk}=k^{-4}. \tag{4}
   \]

Join two events in the dependency graph when their sets of point variables intersect. Assign
\[
x_E=2p^2,\qquad x_F=2k^{-4}
\]
to edge and progression events respectively.

An edge event has two vertices. By (1), it meets at most \(4t\) edge events, and by (2), it meets at most \(2kt\) progression events. Thus the sum of \(x\)-values over its dependencies is at most
\[
S_E\le 4t(2p^2)+2kt(2k^{-4})
     =8tp^2+\frac{4t}{k^3}. \tag{5}
\]

A progression event has \(k\) vertices. It meets at most \(2kt\) edge events and at most \(k^2t\) progression events. Therefore
\[
S_F\le 2kt(2p^2)+k^2t(2k^{-4})
     =4ktp^2+\frac{2t}{k^2}.
\]
Using \(p^2=16(\log k)^2/k^2\),
\[
S_F\le
\frac{64t(\log k)^2}{k}+\frac{2t}{k^2}\le\frac14. \tag{6}
\]
For \(k\ge2\), the right side of (5) is at most the right side of (6), so \(S_E\le1/4\) as well.

For any finite collection \(0\le y_j\le1\),
\[
\prod_j(1-y_j)\ge1-\sum_jy_j.
\]
Thus the product over the dependency neighborhood of either bad event is at least \(3/4\). Consequently,
\[
x_E\prod_{A\sim E_e}(1-x_A)
\ge 2p^2\cdot\frac34
>p^2=\Pr(E_e),
\]
and, using (4),
\[
x_F\prod_{A\sim F_P}(1-x_A)
\ge 2k^{-4}\cdot\frac34
>k^{-4}\ge\Pr(F_P).
\]
Lemma 2 now gives positive probability that no bad event occurs. Such an outcome has no red unit pair and has a red point in every progression. ∎

For every fixed \(t\), condition (3) holds for all sufficiently large \(k\), since \((\log k)^2/k\to0\). Thus Route 3 succeeds completely for finite systems of bounded local directional complexity.

By Boolean compactness, the same statement gives an infinite coloring on any geometric unit-distance system whose finite progression cores all have the same uniform bound \(t\).

### 4. Necessary structure of any finite obstruction

Taking the contrapositive of Theorem 3 gives:

**Corollary 4.**  
If \(k\ge32\) and a finite system is unsatisfiable, then its progression core contains a point incident with more than
\[
\frac{k}{256(\log k)^2+8/k} \tag{7}
\]
distinct unoriented unit directions to other core points.

**Proof.** If \(t\) were no larger than the quantity in (7), then
\[
t\left(\frac{64(\log k)^2}{k}+\frac{2}{k^2}\right)\le\frac14,
\]
and Theorem 3 would apply. ∎

Together with Proposition 1, any large-\(k\) obstruction must therefore have both:

- an odd cycle in its relevant unit-distance graph;
- a relevant point with many distinct unit directions.

These are genuine restrictions, but not sufficient to settle the problem.

### 5. Why the parameter \(t\) cannot simply be bounded geometrically

For every \(T\) and \(k\), there is a finite progression core with \(t\ge T\).

Take \(v=(0,0)\) and choose \(T\) unit vectors \(w_1,\dots,w_T\), no two parallel up to sign. Include a \(k\)-term unit progression through \(v\). For each \(j\), choose any unit vector \(z_j\) and include
\[
w_j,w_j+z_j,\dots,w_j+(k-1)z_j.
\]
All \(w_j\) belong to the progression core, and each is at unit distance from \(v\). Hence
\[
t_C(v)\ge T.
\]

Thus no incidence bound depending only on \(k\) follows from the fact that all points lie in progressions. A successful continuation of the local-lemma argument would have to exploit high-direction vertices rather than exclude them.

### 6. Maximum independent sets do not solve the finite problem

A tempting idea is to take a maximum independent set of the unit-distance graph and hope it meets every sufficiently long progression. This fails in an exact and arbitrarily large geometric example.

**Proposition 5.**  
For every \(n\ge2\), there is a finite \(V\subset\mathbb R^2\) containing an \(n\)-term unit-step progression \(B\) such that the unique maximum independent set of \(G_{\rm unit}[V]\) is disjoint from \(B\).

**Proof.** Let
\[
b_i=(i,0),\qquad 0\le i<n.
\]
These form the required progression.

For each \(i\), choose two points \(\ell_{i,1},\ell_{i,2}\) on the unit circle centered at \(b_i\), subject to:

- neither is at unit distance from any \(b_j\) with \(j\ne i\);
- no two chosen leaf points are at unit distance;
- all chosen points are distinct from the \(b_j\).

These choices can be made inductively. At any stage, a new point is chosen from one fixed circle. Each previously imposed prohibition excludes at most two points of that circle, because two distinct circles meet in at most two points. Only finitely many points are excluded, while the circle is infinite.

The resulting unit-distance graph consists exactly of:

- the path \(b_0b_1\cdots b_{n-1}\);
- two leaf edges \(b_i\ell_{i,1}\) and \(b_i\ell_{i,2}\) at every \(b_i\).

Let
\[
L=\{\ell_{i,j}:0\le i<n,\ j=1,2\}.
\]
Then \(L\) is independent and has size \(2n\).

For any independent set \(I\), its intersection with
\[
\{b_i,\ell_{i,1},\ell_{i,2}\}
\]
has size at most two; if \(b_i\in I\), that intersection has size at most one. Hence \(|I|\le2n\), and equality is possible only if \(I\) contains both leaves and not \(b_i\) for every \(i\). Therefore \(L\) is the unique maximum independent set, and \(L\cap B=\varnothing\). ∎

The construction can use any fixed number \(M\ge2\) of independent private leaves per progression point, so even cardinality objectives with substantial local exchange penalties can be forced to prefer missing the progression.

This does not provide an obstruction to the original coloring problem: a smaller independent set using alternate \(b_i\)'s hits the progression. It specifically refutes maximum-independent-set selection as the missing finite theorem.

### 7. Finite additive phase colorings cannot provide the compactness theorem

Another natural approach is to assign phases through a homomorphism to a finite cyclic group and take one or more phases as red. There is an exact finite directional obstruction to every such construction.

**Proposition 6.**  
For every integer \(m\ge2\), there are three unit vectors \(d,a,b\) such that
\[
d=m(a-b).
\]
Consequently, every homomorphism from their generated additive group to an abelian group of exponent dividing \(m\) sends the unit vector \(d\) to zero.

**Proof.** Set
\[
d=(1,0),
\]
and
\[
a=\left(\frac1{2m},\sqrt{1-\frac1{4m^2}}\right),\qquad
b=\left(-\frac1{2m},\sqrt{1-\frac1{4m^2}}\right).
\]
Then \(\|a\|_2=\|b\|_2=\|d\|_2=1\), while
\[
a-b=\left(\frac1m,0\right),
\]
so \(d=m(a-b)\).

If \(\phi\) maps the generated group into an abelian group of exponent dividing \(m\), then
\[
\phi(d)=m(\phi(a)-\phi(b))=0.
\]
∎

If a coloring is pulled back from such a finite phase map, translation by \(d\) does not change the phase. Any phase declared red gives a red-red unit pair along \(d\); any phase declared blue gives an arbitrarily long blue \(d\)-progression. Allowing different phase offsets on different cosets does not help, because the phase remains constant along every \(d\)-orbit.

Thus a universal proof cannot be obtained from a fixed finite additive quotient or a finite cyclic phase in which every unit vector is required to act nontrivially. This does not rule out nonhomomorphic finite-state constructions.

## Self-Audit

1. **The bounded-direction theorem depends on the precise event-incidence counts.**  
   The most delicate estimates are \(\deg_C(v)\le2t\) and that \(v\) lies in at most \(kt\) progression clauses. They hold because a fixed unoriented direction gives at most two unit neighbors and at most one progression for each possible position of \(v\). Reversed or duplicate clauses must be removed, which does not change satisfiability.

2. **The private-leaf construction is existential rather than given by a closed formula.**  
   Its induction is nevertheless exact: at each step only finitely many points of a circle are forbidden, each arising from an intersection of two distinct circles or an equality with a previously selected point. Therefore a permissible point certainly remains. The computation below can also generate rational examples exactly.

3. **The obstruction diagnosis is only necessary, not close to sufficient.**  
   High local directional complexity and an odd cycle may both occur in easily satisfiable instances. I do not claim otherwise. The unresolved step is precisely a theorem handling high-incidence “hub” points without a bounded-degree hypothesis; proving such a theorem appears comparable in strength to obtaining a finite upper bound for the original problem.

The next code constructs the private-leaf examples using exact rational coordinates. The rational parametrization
\[
\left(\frac{1-s^2}{1+s^2},\frac{2s}{1+s^2}\right)
\]
lies exactly on the unit circle.

```python
from fractions import Fraction as F
from math import gcd
from itertools import count

def add(P, Q):
    return (P[0] + Q[0], P[1] + Q[1])

def sub(P, Q):
    return (P[0] - Q[0], P[1] - Q[1])

def sqdist(P, Q):
    dx, dy = sub(P, Q)
    return dx*dx + dy*dy

def rational_parameters():
    seen = set()
    for H in count(1):
        for den in range(1, H + 1):
            for num in range(-H, H + 1):
                if max(abs(num), den) != H:
                    continue
                if gcd(abs(num), den) != 1:
                    continue
                s = F(num, den)
                if s not in seen:
                    seen.add(s)
                    yield s

def unit_offset(s):
    return (
        (1 - s*s) / (1 + s*s),
        (2*s) / (1 + s*s)
    )

def private_leaf_instance(n, leaves_per_center=2):
    centers = [(F(i), F(0)) for i in range(n)]
    points = list(centers)
    labels = [("b", i, 0) for i in range(n)]
    leaves = []

    for i, center in enumerate(centers):
        made = 0
        for s in rational_parameters():
            candidate = add(center, unit_offset(s))
            if candidate in points:
                continue

            valid = True
            for Q in points:
                if Q == center:
                    continue
                if sqdist(candidate, Q) == 1:
                    valid = False
                    break

            if not valid:
                continue

            assert sqdist(candidate, center) == 1
            points.append(candidate)
            labels.append(("leaf", i, made))
            leaves.append(candidate)
            made += 1
            if made == leaves_per_center:
                break

    # Verify the exact unit graph.
    unit_edges = set()
    for i in range(len(points)):
        for j in range(i + 1, len(points)):
            if sqdist(points[i], points[j]) == 1:
                unit_edges.add((i, j))

    expected = set()
    for i in range(n - 1):
        expected.add((i, i + 1))
    for idx, label in enumerate(labels):
        if label[0] == "leaf":
            expected.add(tuple(sorted((label[1], idx))))

    assert unit_edges == expected
    return points, labels, unit_edges

points, labels, edges = private_leaf_instance(5, 2)
print("vertices:", len(points))
print("unit edges:", len(edges))
```

The following exact brute-force check confirms uniqueness of the maximum independent set for small instances.

```python
def independent_masks(num_vertices, edges):
    edge_masks = [(1 << i) | (1 << j) for i, j in edges]
    for mask in range(1 << num_vertices):
        if all((mask & em) != em for em in edge_masks):
            yield mask

n = 4
points, labels, edges = private_leaf_instance(n, 2)
N = len(points)

best_size = -1
best_masks = []
for mask in independent_masks(N, edges):
    size = mask.bit_count()
    if size > best_size:
        best_size = size
        best_masks = [mask]
    elif size == best_size:
        best_masks.append(mask)

leaf_mask = 0
for i, label in enumerate(labels):
    if label[0] == "leaf":
        leaf_mask |= 1 << i

assert best_size == 2*n
assert best_masks == [leaf_mask]
assert all((leaf_mask & (1 << i)) == 0 for i in range(n))
print("unique maximum independent set verified")
```

Exact progression extraction and SAT-style checking can be added as follows.

```python
def exact_progressions(points, k):
    point_index = {P: i for i, P in enumerate(points)}
    progressions = set()

    for i, X in enumerate(points):
        for j, Y in enumerate(points):
            if i == j or sqdist(X, Y) != 1:
                continue
            u = sub(Y, X)
            seq = []
            ok = True
            for r in range(k):
                Z = (X[0] + r*u[0], X[1] + r*u[1])
                if Z not in point_index:
                    ok = False
                    break
                seq.append(point_index[Z])
            if ok:
                # Reversal gives the same clause.
                progressions.add(tuple(sorted(seq)))

    return sorted(progressions)

def admissible_masks(points, edges, k):
    progs = exact_progressions(points, k)
    edge_masks = [(1 << i) | (1 << j) for i, j in edges]
    prog_masks = [sum(1 << i for i in P) for P in progs]

    for red in range(1 << len(points)):
        if any((red & e) == e for e in edge_masks):
            continue
        if any((red & P) == 0 for P in prog_masks):
            continue
        yield red

points, labels, edges = private_leaf_instance(4, 2)
progs = exact_progressions(points, 4)
print("4-term progressions:", progs)
print("number of admissible colorings:", sum(
    1 for _ in admissible_masks(points, edges, 4)
))
```

## Route Diagnosis

**Proved ledger**

- Points outside the union of progression clauses may be colored blue and deleted.
- A bipartite progression core is always satisfiable.
- Bounded local directional complexity \(t\) implies satisfiability whenever
  \[
  64t(\log k)^2/k+2t/k^2\le1/4.
  \]
- Therefore every large-\(k\) finite obstruction must contain both an odd unit-distance cycle and a point with \(\Omega(k/\log^2 k)\) relevant unit directions.
- Maximum independent sets can uniquely avoid arbitrarily long unit-step progressions.
- Fixed finite additive quotient/phase constructions are obstructed by exact relations \(d=m(a-b)\) among unit vectors.

**Plausible but unproved**

- A high-incidence reduction might branch on a point lying in many relevant directions: coloring such a hub red simultaneously hits all progressions through it, while coloring it blue should simplify those clauses. No potential function or induction currently controls the unit-neighbor clauses created in the red branch.
- A variable-probability or cluster-expansion local lemma might handle hubs better than the uniform random coloring above. I found no choice of local probabilities giving uniform estimates without effectively assuming the desired high-incidence reduction.
- Minimal unsatisfiable cores may have stronger geometric structure than arbitrary progression cores, but no proved lemma currently bounds or organizes their high-degree vertices.

**Dead ends**

- Arbitrary maximal or maximum independent sets do not hit long progressions; Proposition 5 gives exact counterexamples.
- A uniform local-lemma argument based only on maximum degree or maximum direction count cannot apply directly because progression cores can have arbitrarily large local direction count.
- Finite cyclic or finite-group phase homomorphisms cannot make every unit direction nontrivial; Proposition 6 gives a three-vector obstruction for every modulus.
- Bipartiteness and ordinary bounded chromatic number are too weak: bipartiteness solves the finite instance, but selecting one class from a \(q\)-coloring need not meet every progression.

**Precise block**

Route 3 now reduces to handling high-direction hub points in finite progression cores. The bounded-direction theorem covers the sparse side, but there is no rigorous reduction showing that every high-direction finite system is satisfiable or can be decomposed into bounded-direction systems. Establishing such a reduction would already give a finite upper bound for Erdős Problem #188 and appears to be of comparable difficulty to the original open problem.

A fresh attempt should combine exact SAT searches with the structural filters above: prune to the progression core, discard bipartite cases, and deliberately generate configurations with odd cycles and hub direction count near or above the threshold in (7). Unsatisfiable cores could reveal the missing hub gadget; persistent satisfiability may instead suggest a constructive hub-elimination lemma.
