STATUS: BLOCKED

## Result

Route 1 yields nontrivial unconditional structure for the harmonic zero sets \(S_p\), but not enough to prove infinitely many coprime cases. I prove: (i) every non-forced zero is encoded by a prime divisor \(p\ge 2d+1\) of the reduced numerator of \(H_d\); (ii) zeros satisfy a spacing inequality implying that every interval of \(N\) digits contains \(O(N^{2/3})\) zeros; (iii) the weighted local hazard satisfies
\[
W_p:=\sum_{d\in S_p}\log\!\left(1+\frac1d\right)
\ll (\log p)^{-1/3};
\]
and (iv) the stronger dyadic average estimate
\[
\sum_{P<p\le2P}W_p\ll \frac{P^{3/4}}{\log P},
\qquad
\sum_{P<p\le2P}\frac{W_p}{\log p}
\ll \frac{P^{3/4}}{(\log P)^2}.
\]
These are genuine power-saving bounds over elementary estimates, but they remain far above the random-model scale and give no control over correlations between different bases. Thus the unresolved infinitude of \(g_n=1\) remains blocked.

## Complete Argument

### 1. Prime-divisor parametrization of all non-forced zeros

Write
\[
H_d=\frac{U_d}{V_d}
\]
in lowest terms, and put \(H_0=0\). For an odd prime \(p\), define
\[
Z_p:=\{d\in\{0,\dots,p-1\}:H_d\equiv0\pmod p\}.
\]
Thus
\[
Z_p=\{0\}\cup S_p.
\]

The symmetry from the brief extends to all \(0\le d\le p-1\):
\[
H_{p-1-d}\equiv H_d\pmod p.
\]
Indeed,
\[
H_{p-1-d}
=H_{p-1}-\sum_{j=p-d}^{p-1}\frac1j
\equiv-\sum_{r=1}^{d}\frac1{p-r}
\equiv H_d\pmod p.
\]

Define the small-root set
\[
T_p:=\left\{1\le d\le\frac{p-1}{2}:p\mid U_d\right\}.
\]
Since \(d<p\), the reduced denominator \(V_d\) is prime to \(p\), so
\[
p\mid U_d\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad H_d\equiv0\pmod p.
\]

It follows that
\[
S_p=\{p-1\}\cup T_p\cup\{p-1-d:d\in T_p\},                     \tag{1}
\]
where the middle root \(d=(p-1)/2\), if present, is counted only once in the union.

To verify that (1) is exhaustive, take \(x\in S_p\setminus\{p-1\}\). Then \(1\le x\le p-2\), and
\[
d:=\min(x,p-1-x)
\]
lies in \(1\le d\le(p-1)/2\). By symmetry \(H_d\equiv0\pmod p\), hence \(p\mid U_d\).

Thus every non-forced zero is generated by an incidence
\[
p\mid U_d,\qquad p\ge2d+1.                                  \tag{2}
\]

For example,
\[
H_3=\frac{11}{6},\qquad H_5=\frac{137}{60},\qquad
H_8=\frac{761}{280},
\]
so these produce, respectively, zeros
\[
3,7\in S_{11},\qquad 5,131\in S_{137},\qquad
8,752\in S_{761}.
\]

This parametrization is the central arithmetic content of Route 1: controlling \(S_p\) on average requires controlling large prime divisors of the sequence \(U_d\).

---

### 2. The midpoint and Wieferich primes

The symmetry has a fixed point at \(d=(p-1)/2\). This point cannot simply be discarded.

Let
\[
q_p(2):=\frac{2^{p-1}-1}{p}\pmod p
\]
be the Fermat quotient. Then
\[
H_{(p-1)/2}\equiv-2q_p(2)\pmod p.                            \tag{3}
\]

To prove this, for \(1\le k\le p-1\),
\[
\binom pk
=\frac pk\binom{p-1}{k-1}
\equiv \frac p k(-1)^{k-1}\pmod{p^2}.
\]
Therefore
\[
2^p-2=\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\binom pk
\equiv p\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}k\pmod{p^2}.
\]
Dividing by \(p\) gives
\[
2q_p(2)\equiv\sum_{k=1}^{p-1}\frac{(-1)^{k-1}}k\pmod p.
\]
If \(E\) is the sum over even \(k\), then
\[
E=\frac12H_{(p-1)/2}.
\]
Since \(H_{p-1}\equiv0\pmod p\), the sum over odd \(k\) equals \(-E\). Hence the alternating sum equals
\[
-E-E=-H_{(p-1)/2},
\]
which proves (3).

Consequently,
\[
\frac{p-1}{2}\in S_p
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
p\text{ is a base-}2\text{ Wieferich prime}.                 \tag{4}
\]
Thus a proof that all exceptional zeros occur in distinct reflected pairs would be false; \(p=1093\) already gives a fixed midpoint zero.

### 3. A spacing inequality for harmonic zeros

The next lemma gives a uniform restriction on how densely zeros can occur.

#### Lemma 1

For a prime \(p\) and \(1\le\ell\le p-1\), the number of integers
\[
a\in\{0,\dots,p-1-\ell\}
\]
such that both \(a\) and \(a+\ell\) belong to \(Z_p\) is at most \(\ell-1\).

#### Proof

If \(a,a+\ell\in Z_p\), then
\[
0\equiv H_{a+\ell}-H_a
=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\frac1{a+i}\pmod p.
\]
All denominators are nonzero modulo \(p\). Multiplication by
\[
\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}(a+j)
\]
shows that \(a\) is a root of
\[
R_\ell(X):=
\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}
\prod_{\substack{1\le j\le\ell\\j\ne i}}(X+j)
\in\mathbb F_p[X].
\]
Each summand is monic of degree \(\ell-1\), so the coefficient of
\(X^{\ell-1}\) in \(R_\ell\) is \(\ell\), which is nonzero modulo \(p\).
Thus \(R_\ell\) is a nonzero polynomial of degree exactly \(\ell-1\), and it has at most \(\ell-1\) roots in \(\mathbb F_p\). ∎

This estimate is sharp in small examples. For \(p=11\),
\[
Z_{11}=\{0,3,7,10\};
\]
there are two zero pairs at distance \(3\), namely \((0,3)\) and \((7,10)\), attaining the bound \(\ell-1=2\).

### 4. Interval sparsity: \(O(N^{2/3})\) zeros

#### Lemma 2

Let \(I\subseteq\{0,\dots,p-1\}\) be an interval containing \(N\) integers. Then
\[
\#(Z_p\cap I)\le 7N^{2/3}.                                  \tag{5}
\]

#### Proof

Put
\[
m:=\#(Z_p\cap I).
\]
There is nothing to prove if \(m<8\), since \(m\le7\le7N^{2/3}\).

Assume \(m\ge8\), and set
\[
L:=\left\lceil\frac{4N}{m}\right\rceil.
\]
Partition \(I\) into \(r=\lceil N/L\rceil\) consecutive blocks, each containing at most \(L\) integers. Since \(L\ge4N/m\),
\[
r\le\frac NL+1\le\frac m4+1\le\frac{3m}{8}.
\]

If the blocks contain \(m_1,\dots,m_r\) zeros, the number of pairs of zeros lying in the same block is
\[
\sum_{i=1}^r\binom{m_i}{2}
=\frac12\left(\sum_i m_i^2-m\right)
\ge\frac12\left(\frac{m^2}{r}-m\right)
\ge\frac{5m}{6}.                                             \tag{6}
\]

Every such pair has a difference \(\ell\) with \(1\le\ell\le L-1\).
By Lemma 1, the total number of zero pairs with difference \(\ell\) is at most \(\ell-1\). Therefore
\[
\sum_i\binom{m_i}{2}
\le\sum_{\ell=1}^{L-1}(\ell-1)
<\frac{L^2}{2}.                                              \tag{7}
\]

Since \(m\le N\),
\[
L\le\frac{4N}{m}+1\le\frac{5N}{m}.
\]
Combining (6) and (7),
\[
\frac{5m}{6}\le\frac{25N^2}{2m^2},
\]
so
\[
m^3\le15N^2.
\]
Hence
\[
m\le15^{1/3}N^{2/3}<3N^{2/3}<7N^{2/3}.
\]
This proves (5). ∎

In particular,
\[
|S_p|+1=|Z_p|\ll p^{2/3}.                                   \tag{8}
\]

More importantly, (5) controls zeros close to the beginning of the digit range, where the leading-digit weight \(1/d\) is largest.

### 5. An exponential bound for harmonic numerators

We need a uniform lower bound on the position of a small zero.

#### Lemma 3

For every \(d\ge1\),
\[
\log U_d\le13d.                                              \tag{9}
\]

#### Proof

Let
\[
\vartheta(x):=\sum_{q\le x}\log q
\]
over primes \(q\), and let
\[
\psi(x):=\sum_{q^k\le x}\log q.
\]
For an integer \(n\ge2\), the central binomial coefficient gives
\[
\vartheta(n)-\vartheta(\lfloor n/2\rfloor)\le2n.
\]
Indeed, for \(n=2m\), every prime \(m<q\le2m\) divides
\(\binom{2m}{m}<2^{2m}\). For \(n=2m+1\), the same argument handles
\(m<q\le2m\), and the possible prime \(2m+1\) contributes at most
\(\log(2m+1)\); the displayed crude bound follows.

Iteration gives
\[
\vartheta(n)\le4n.
\]
Since
\[
\psi(d)=\sum_{k\le\log_2 d}\vartheta(d^{1/k}),
\]
we get
\[
\psi(d)
\le4d+4\sqrt d\,\log_2d
\le12d,
\]
using \(\log_2d\le2\sqrt d\).

Now \(V_d\mid L_d\), so
\[
\log V_d\le\log L_d=\psi(d)\le12d.
\]
Also \(H_d\le d\), and hence
\[
U_d=H_dV_d\le dL_d.
\]
Thus
\[
\log U_d\le\log d+12d\le13d.
\]
∎

Consequently, if \(d\in T_p\), then \(p\mid U_d\), so \(U_d\ge p\), and therefore
\[
d\ge\frac{\log p}{13}.                                      \tag{10}
\]

### 6. Uniform weighted bound for \(S_p\)

Define
\[
W_p:=\sum_{d\in S_p}\log\left(1+\frac1d\right).
\]

#### Theorem 4

There is an absolute constant \(C\) such that for every prime \(p\),
\[
W_p\le C(\log p)^{-1/3}.                                    \tag{11}
\]

#### Proof

The result is immediate for \(p=2\), since \(S_2=\varnothing\). Let \(p\) be odd.

By (1), and using \(\log(1+x)\le x\),
\[
W_p
\le\frac1{p-1}
 +2\sum_{d\in T_p}\frac1d.                                  \tag{12}
\]
The factor \(2\) harmlessly overcounts a possible midpoint root.

Suppose first that \(\log p\ge13\), and put
\[
r:=\frac{\log p}{13}\ge1.
\]
By (10), every \(d\in T_p\) satisfies \(d\ge r\).

Divide the possible \(d\)'s into dyadic intervals
\[
2^kr\le d<2^{k+1}r,\qquad k\ge0.
\]
Such an interval contains \(O(2^kr)\) integers, and Lemma 2 gives at most
\[
O\bigl((2^kr)^{2/3}\bigr)
\]
zeros. Its contribution to \(\sum_{d\in T_p}1/d\) is therefore
\[
O\left(\frac{(2^kr)^{2/3}}{2^kr}\right)
=O\left(r^{-1/3}2^{-k/3}\right).
\]
Summing the convergent geometric series,
\[
\sum_{d\in T_p}\frac1d
\ll r^{-1/3}
\ll(\log p)^{-1/3}.                                         \tag{13}
\]
Equations (12) and (13) prove (11) for \(\log p\ge13\).

For the remaining finitely bounded range \(\log p<13\), the same dyadic argument starting at \(r=1\) gives \(W_p=O(1)\). Enlarging the absolute constant proves (11) for all \(p\). ∎

The logarithmic density within one base-\(p\) period of the bad digit set is exactly
\[
\delta_p:=\frac{W_p}{\log p}.
\]
Thus
\[
\delta_p\ll(\log p)^{-4/3}.                                 \tag{14}
\]
This is a uniform decay statement, but its sum over primes still diverges extremely rapidly.

### 7. A stronger dyadic average theorem

The preceding bound can be improved substantially on average over primes.

#### Theorem 5

For \(P\ge3\),
\[
\sum_{P<p\le2P}W_p
\ll\frac{P^{3/4}}{\log P},                                  \tag{15}
\]
and consequently
\[
\sum_{P<p\le2P}\frac{W_p}{\log p}
\ll\frac{P^{3/4}}{(\log P)^2}.                              \tag{16}
\]

#### Proof

For a dyadic integer \(D\), let \(I(P,D)\) count pairs \((p,d)\) satisfying
\[
P<p\le2P,\qquad D\le d<2D,\qquad
d\le\frac{p-1}{2},\qquad p\mid U_d.
\]

There are two independent upper bounds for \(I(P,D)\).

First, for each fixed \(p\), Lemma 2 shows that the interval
\([D,2D)\) contains \(O(D^{2/3})\) roots. The elementary Chebyshev bound
\[
\pi(2P)\ll\frac P{\log P}
\]
therefore gives
\[
I(P,D)\ll\frac{P D^{2/3}}{\log P}.                           \tag{17}
\]

Second, fix \(d\in[D,2D)\). If \(k\) primes in \((P,2P]\) divide \(U_d\), their product divides \(U_d\). Lemma 3 gives
\[
P^k\le U_d\le e^{13d}<e^{26D},
\]
and hence
\[
k\le\frac{26D}{\log P}.
\]
There are at most \(D\) integers \(d\in[D,2D)\), so
\[
I(P,D)\ll\frac{D^2}{\log P}.                                \tag{18}
\]

Combining (17) and (18),
\[
I(P,D)\ll
\frac1{\log P}\min\bigl(PD^{2/3},D^2\bigr).                 \tag{19}
\]

Because \(d\le(p-1)/2<P\), only dyadic \(D\le P\) occur. Therefore
\[
\begin{aligned}
\sum_{P<p\le2P}\sum_{d\in T_p}\frac1d
&\le \sum_{\substack{D\le P\\D\ {\rm dyadic}}}
       \frac{I(P,D)}{D}\\
&\ll\frac1{\log P}
\sum_{\substack{D\le P\\D\ {\rm dyadic}}}
\min\bigl(PD^{-1/3},D\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\]
The two terms are equal when \(D=P^{3/4}\). For \(D\le P^{3/4}\), the minimum is \(D\), and the dyadic sum is \(O(P^{3/4})\). For \(D>P^{3/4}\), it is \(PD^{-1/3}\), whose dyadic sum is again \(O(P^{3/4})\). Hence
\[
\sum_{P<p\le2P}\sum_{d\in T_p}\frac1d
\ll\frac{P^{3/4}}{\log P}.                                  \tag{20}
\]

By (12), the contribution of all non-forced roots satisfies the same bound. The forced roots contribute
\[
\sum_{P<p\le2P}\log\left(1+\frac1{p-1}\right)
\ll\frac1P\pi(2P)
\ll\frac1{\log P},
\]
which is absorbed into (15). Finally, \(\log p\ge\log P\) in this block, so (16) follows from (15). ∎

For comparison, the random model predicts that the left side of (16) should be only about \(1/\log P\). The proved bound is larger by approximately
\[
\frac{P^{3/4}}{\log P}.
\]
It is therefore far from the scale needed for a dimension-one sieve.

### 8. Why the forced zero alone cannot explain the heuristic

The forced digit \(p-1\) contributes
\[
\delta_p^{\mathrm{forced}}
=
\frac{\log(p/(p-1))}{\log p}
\ll\frac1{p\log p}.
\]
The sum over primes converges. Indeed, in a dyadic block,
\[
\sum_{P<p\le2P}\frac1{p\log p}
\ll \frac{\pi(2P)}{P\log P}
\ll\frac1{(\log P)^2},
\]
and summing over \(P=2^k\) gives a convergent series.

Thus any sieve-dimension-one behavior must come from the exceptional incidences \(p\mid U_d\) with \(p\ge2d+1\), not from the universal digit \(p-1\).

### 9. The already-settled non-coprime half

For every \(e\ge1\), take
\[
n=2\cdot3^e.
\]
Then the leading base-\(3\) digit is \(2\), and
\[
H_2=\frac32\equiv0\pmod3.
\]
The exact local criterion gives
\[
3\mid g_n.
\]
Hence \(\{n:g_n>1\}\) is infinite.

No argument above proves that \(\{n:g_n=1\}\) is infinite.

### 10. Ledger

**Proved lemmas**

1. Every non-forced zero of \(S_p\) is generated by a pair \(p\mid U_d\), \(p\ge2d+1\).
2. The midpoint root is equivalent to \(p\) being base-\(2\) Wieferich.
3. At a fixed separation \(\ell\), there are at most \(\ell-1\) pairs of harmonic zeros.
4. Every interval of \(N\) digits contains \(O(N^{2/3})\) zeros.
5. \(\log U_d\le13d\).
6. \(W_p\ll(\log p)^{-1/3}\).
7. Dyadically,
   \[
   \sum_{P<p\le2P}W_p\ll P^{3/4}/\log P.
   \]

**Plausible but unproved claims**

1. For
   \[
   I(P,D)=\#\{(p,d):P<p\le2P,\ D\le d<2D,\ p\mid U_d\},
   \]
   the random-scale estimate should be approximately
   \[
   I(P,D)\asymp\frac{D}{\log P}
   \]
   when \(D\) is not too small.
2. Suitable joint estimates for different primes should lead to a dimension-one sieve and \(C(x)\asymp x/\log x\).
3. The spacing bound likely has substantial additional arithmetic strengthening, but none was found.

**Dead ends**

1. Bounding prime divisors of each \(U_d\) only through \(\log U_d=O(d)\) gives \(I(P,D)\ll D^2/\log P\), much too large.
2. Spacing alone gives the exponent \(2/3\); combining it with numerator size improves the average only to exponent \(3/4\), still far from the random scale.
3. A union bound using \(W_p\) cannot work: even the proved average upper bound is non-summable by an enormous margin.
4. Local bounds for individual \(S_p\) do not control correlations among the leading-digit events for different bases.
5. Treating all non-forced zeros as reflected pairs without a fixed point fails because of Wieferich primes.

## Self-Audit

1. **The interval-sparsity exponent \(2/3\).**  
   The delicate point is converting the separation bound into a density bound. The block-pair argument includes all pairs within each block, and Lemma 1 bounds the total number of pairs at each difference globally, so there is no omitted multiplicity. The constants are deliberately loose.

2. **The numerator bound \(\log U_d\le13d\).**  
   This depends on an elementary Chebyshev estimate for \(\vartheta\) and the identity \(\log L_d=\psi(d)\). Both were proved at a sufficiently crude level; no prime number theorem or unproved hypothesis is being used.

3. **The relevance of the dyadic average estimate.**  
   This is the weakest point strategically, not logically. The estimate is rigorous, but it does not imply any survivors because it remains far above the expected scale and says nothing about cross-prime correlations. I have not used it to claim progress toward infinitude beyond identifying a sharper quantitative obstruction.

## Computations To Verify

```python
from fractions import Fraction
from math import log, log1p, gcd, lcm
from sympy import primerange

def zero_set(p):
    """Z_p = {0 <= d <= p-1 : H_d == 0 mod p}."""
    h = 0
    Z = [0]
    for d in range(1, p):
        h = (h + pow(d, -1, p)) % p
        if h == 0:
            Z.append(d)
    return Z

def S_set(p):
    return [d for d in zero_set(p) if d != 0]

def check_spacing(B=5000):
    """
    Verifies:
    number of zero pairs at distance ell <= ell-1.
    """
    for p in primerange(2, B + 1):
        Z = set(zero_set(p))
        for ell in range(1, p):
            count = sum(
                1 for a in range(0, p - ell)
                if a in Z and a + ell in Z
            )
            assert count <= ell - 1, (p, ell, count)
    return True

def check_interval_bound(B=1000):
    """
    Exhaustively checks the loose bound
    #zeros in an interval of N positions <= 7*N^(2/3).
    """
    for p in primerange(2, B + 1):
        Z = set(zero_set(p))
        for a in range(p):
            count = 0
            for b in range(a, p):
                if b in Z:
                    count += 1
                N = b - a + 1
                assert count <= 7 * (N ** (2/3)) + 1e-12, (
                    p, a, b, count
                )
    return True

def harmonic_numerators(D):
    H = Fraction(0, 1)
    U = [0] * (D + 1)
    for d in range(1, D + 1):
        H += Fraction(1, d)
        U[d] = H.numerator
    return U

def check_prime_divisor_parametrization(B=2000):
    U = harmonic_numerators(B)
    for p in primerange(3, B + 1):
        Z = set(zero_set(p))
        T = {
            d for d in range(1, (p - 1)//2 + 1)
            if U[d] % p == 0
        }
        generated = {p - 1} | T | {p - 1 - d for d in T}
        assert generated == Z - {0}, (p, generated, Z - {0})
    return True

def check_midpoint_wieferich(B=100000):
    for p in primerange(3, B + 1):
        Z = set(zero_set(p))
        mid = (p - 1)//2
        r = pow(2, p - 1, p*p)
        q = ((r - 1)//p) % p
        assert ((mid in Z) == (q == 0)), (p, mid, q)
    return True

def W(p):
    return sum(log1p(1/d) for d in S_set(p))

def zero_statistics(B=100000):
    max_size = (0, None)
    max_W = (0.0, None)
    exceptional = []
    for p in primerange(2, B + 1):
        S = S_set(p)
        wp = sum(log1p(1/d) for d in S)
        if len(S) > max_size[0]:
            max_size = (len(S), p)
        if wp > max_W[0]:
            max_W = (wp, p)
        if p > 2 and S != [p - 1]:
            exceptional.append((p, S))
    return {
        "max_size": max_size,
        "max_W": max_W,
        "first_exceptional": exceptional[:20],
    }

def dyadic_statistics(P):
    """
    The theorem predicts bounded growth of:
      sum W_p * log(P) / P^(3/4)
      sum (W_p/log p) * log(P)^2 / P^(3/4).
    """
    primes = list(primerange(P + 1, 2*P + 1))
    sum_W = sum(W(p) for p in primes)
    sum_delta = sum(W(p)/log(p) for p in primes)
    return {
        "P": P,
        "number_of_primes": len(primes),
        "sum_W": sum_W,
        "scaled_W": sum_W * log(P) / (P ** 0.75),
        "sum_delta": sum_delta,
        "scaled_delta":
            sum_delta * (log(P) ** 2) / (P ** 0.75),
    }

def incidence(P, D):
    """
    I(P,D) from Theorem 5, computed through modular zero sets.
    """
    total = 0
    pairs = []
    for p in primerange(P + 1, 2*P + 1):
        Z = set(zero_set(p))
        for d in range(D, 2*D):
            if d <= (p - 1)//2 and d in Z:
                total += 1
                pairs.append((p, d))
    b1 = P * (D ** (2/3)) / log(P)
    b2 = D * D / log(P)
    return {
        "I": total,
        "pairs": pairs,
        "spacing_bound_scale": b1,
        "numerator_bound_scale": b2,
        "combined_scale": min(b1, b2),
        "random_scale": D / log(P),
    }

def interval_sieve(X):
    bad = [False] * (X + 1)
    witness = [None] * (X + 1)

    for p in primerange(2, X + 1):
        S = S_set(p)
        q = p
        while q <= X:
            for d in S:
                lo = d * q
                if lo > X:
                    break
                hi = min((d + 1) * q - 1, X)
                for n in range(lo, hi + 1):
                    bad[n] = True
                    if witness[n] is None:
                        witness[n] = p
            q *= p
    return bad, witness

def direct_crosscheck(X=500):
    bad, witness = interval_sieve(X)
    H = Fraction(0, 1)
    L = 1

    for n in range(1, X + 1):
        H += Fraction(1, n)
        L = lcm(L, n)
        assert L % H.denominator == 0
        g = L // H.denominator
        assert (g > 1) == bad[n], (
            n, H, L, g, witness[n]
        )
    return True
```

5. Run `direct_crosscheck` to ensure all interval endpoints and prime-power boundaries are handled correctly.

## Route Diagnosis

Route 1 did reveal a usable arithmetic structure: exceptional zeros are precisely large-prime-divisor incidences \(p\mid U_d\), and these incidences cannot cluster arbitrarily within one \(S_p\). The spacing-polynomial argument gives the first substantial bound here, leading to uniform weighted decay and a dyadic \(P^{3/4}\) power saving.

The route is nevertheless blocked at the incidence problem
\[
I(P,D)=\#\{(p,d):P<p\le2P,\ D\le d<2D,\ p\mid U_d\}.
\]
The present argument proves only
\[
I(P,D)\ll
\frac{\min(PD^{2/3},D^2)}{\log P},
\]
whereas random behavior predicts roughly \(D/\log P\). Closing this polynomial gap would require genuinely new information on the distribution of large prime factors of harmonic numerators, not merely their size. Even such a local theorem would still have to be supplemented by cross-prime joint estimates or a deterministic survivor construction.

A fresh attempt should therefore try one of two things:

1. develop a large-sieve or bilinear estimate for the double sequence \(p\mid U_d\), exploiting the recurrence for \(d!H_d\) and the interval-sum polynomials \(R_\ell\); or
2. combine the proven interval sparsity of \(S_p\) with Route 4, seeking a multiscale survivor lemma that avoids needing full logarithmic equidistribution.

Without one of these additional mechanisms, Route 1 alone does not reach the Erdős problem.
